Module 1.1 — NSTP Law, Bill of Rights & Good Citizenship

＋

1987 Constitution: Bill of Rights

The Bill of Rights is Article III of the 1987 Constitution. It has 22 sections declaring the rights of every Filipino citizen.

  • Sec. 1: due process and equal protection of the laws.
  • Sec. 2: no unreasonable searches and seizures; warrants only on probable cause determined personally by a judge.
  • Sec. 3: privacy of communication; illegally obtained evidence is inadmissible.
  • Sec. 4: freedom of speech, expression, the press, peaceful assembly and petition.
  • Sec. 5: freedom of religion; no state religion; no religious test for civil or political rights.
  • Sec. 6: liberty of abode and the right to travel.
  • Sec. 7: right to information on matters of public concern.
  • Sec. 8: right to form unions, associations or societies.
  • Sec. 9: no taking of private property for public use without just compensation.
  • Sec. 10: no law impairing the obligation of contracts.
  • Sec. 11: free access to courts and legal assistance can't be denied because of poverty.
  • Sec. 12: rights of a person under investigation: to remain silent and to have competent, independent counsel. These can only be waived in writing and in the presence of counsel. No torture or secret detention.
  • Sec. 13: right to bail (except offenses punishable by reclusion perpetua when evidence of guilt is strong); no excessive bail.
  • Sec. 14: due process in criminal cases; presumption of innocence; speedy, impartial, public trial.
  • Sec. 15: the writ of habeas corpus can be suspended only for invasion or rebellion when public safety requires it.
  • Sec. 16: speedy disposition of cases.
  • Sec. 17: no one can be compelled to be a witness against himself (self-incrimination).
  • Sec. 18: no detention solely for political beliefs; no involuntary servitude except as punishment for a crime.
  • Sec. 19: no excessive fines or cruel, degrading, inhuman punishment; death penalty only if Congress provides for heinous crimes.
  • Sec. 20: no imprisonment for debt or non-payment of a poll tax.
  • Sec. 21: no double jeopardy; conviction or acquittal under a law or an ordinance bars prosecution under the other for the same act.
  • Sec. 22: no ex post facto law or bill of attainder.

Suffrage

Suffrage is not in the Bill of Rights; it has its own article. The slides label it Article IV (in the actual Constitution it is Article V). Requirements: a Filipino citizen not disqualified by law, at least 18, resided in the Philippines for at least 1 year and in the voting place for at least 6 months before the election. No literacy, property or other substantive requirement may be imposed.

Rights and duties

Our rights are not “for free.” Every right comes with corresponding duties and responsibilities. Teresita Baltazar: good governance is not enough, we must work on being good citizens by turning the values in the Preamble into concrete action.

RA 9163: NSTP Act of 2001

Sec. 2, Declaration of Policy: the prime duty of government is to serve and protect its citizens. In turn, citizens are responsible for defending the security of the State, and the government may require personal, military or civil service.

The State recognizes the youth's vital role in nation-building and promotes civic consciousness, patriotism and nationalism. The youth, the most valuable resource of the nation, shall be motivated, trained, organized and mobilized in military training, literacy, civic welfare and similar endeavors.

NSTP components

  • ROTC: Reserve Officers' Training Corps
  • LTS: Literacy Training Service
  • CWTS: Civic Welfare Training Service

Good Citizenship Values (CHED MO No. 26)

  • Pagkamaka-Diyos: faith in the Almighty, respect for life, order, work, concern for family and future generations.
  • Pagkamaka-tao: love, freedom, peace, truth, justice.
  • Pagkamaka-bayan: unity, equality, respect for law and government, patriotism, promotion of the common good.
  • Pagkamaka-kalikasan: concern for the environment.

Module 1.2 — Human Rights: A Global Perspective

＋

Before the UDHR

Human rights were not new. Some recognition of human dignity already existed in different cultures and religions, and in some states such as the USA, Great Britain and France.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

  • Proclaimed by the UN General Assembly in Paris on December 10, 1948.
  • A “standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations.”
  • Permanent but non-binding.
  • Global and regional application; covers all facets of a dignified life.
  • Inclusive, notably of women's rights; women helped shape the declaration.

Mechanisms and instruments

  • Treaty-based mechanisms: independent committees.
  • Charter-based mechanisms: the Human Rights Council.
  • Instruments: legal frameworks for upholding human rights (listed on the OHCHR website).

Module 2.1 — Development, Human Rights & Sustainable Development

＋

Traditional view of development

  • Economic growth leads to social progress.
  • Western models are universally applicable.
  • Culture and tradition are obstacles to development.
  • Nature exists primarily as a resource to be exploited.

Contemporary view

  • Amartya Sen: freedom to become what we value.
  • Alain de Janvry & Elizabeth Sadoulet: enhancement of well-being.

Human rights and development

Not new concepts. Global events and trends legitimized them through international laws and treaties. They are interwoven concepts.

Sustainable development

More than a buzzword, it is both analytical (understanding pressing complex issues) and normative (moral and ethical guidelines).

Timeline

  • 1972 Limits to Growth: commissioned by the Club of Rome; UN Conference on the Human Environment in Stockholm.
  • 1987 Brundtland Report (Our Common Future): development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the needs of future generations.
  • 1992 UN Conference on Environment and Development: the Rio Earth Summit; Multilateral Environmental Agreements.
  • 1997 Kyoto Protocol: turned UNFCCC principles into concrete emissions targets (5% below 1990 levels).
  • 2000 Millennium Development Goals: integrated the 3 pillars (economic, social, environmental) as mutually reinforcing.
  • 2012 UN Conference on Sustainable Development: a universal set of goals for urgent environmental, political and economic challenges.
  • 2015 SDGs: 17 goals, adopted August 2015 per the slides (formally adopted by the UN General Assembly in September 2015).

Module 2.2 — SDGs 1, 3 & 16 · Issue Focus: Drug Abuse

＋

SDG 1: No Poverty

End poverty in all its forms everywhere. Target 1.1 originally used $1.25/day; the International Poverty Line is now US$3.00/day.

Agents: DSWD (4Ps), DepDev, DepEd; NGOs like International Care Ministries, Gawad Kalinga, Bantu PH, CARD-MRI, CODE-NGO, Uplift Microfinance, AJ Kalinga, SNPP.

SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being

Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages. Key target for this module: 3.5, strengthen prevention and treatment of substance abuse. Others: 3.4 mental health, 3.6 halve road deaths, 3.8 universal health coverage.

Agents: DOH (PhilHealth, local health centers), DSWD; NGOs like Roots of Health PH, FHI 360, Child Hope Philippines.

SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions

Peaceful, inclusive societies, access to justice, and effective, accountable institutions. Examples: 16.5 reduce corruption and bribery, 16.9 legal identity and birth registration, 16.10 public access to information.

Agents: DOJ, PNP, AFP, NTF-ELCAC, anti-drug abuse offices, COA, CHR; NGOs like Active Vista, Mindanao Peacebuilding Institute, FLAG, CyberGuardians PH.

Issue focus: drug abuse

Drug use disorder (WHO) covers two health conditions: a harmful pattern of drug use and drug dependence. It has psychosocial, environmental and biological determinants. Scientifically it is a treatable health condition, not so much a self-acquired bad habit.

Film: Walang Hanggang Gabi (ABS-CBN) on the war on drugs.

CADAO (Caloocan Anti-Drug Abuse Office) runs CARROTS: Community Assisted Recovery and Rehabilitation Training Services. It came from Bishop Ambo David's call to respect the dignity of life, and is an agreement among the Church, the State (Caloocan LGU), and private individuals and firms.

Module 2.4 — DRRM · SDGs 2, 12 & 13

＋

There is no such thing as a natural disaster

  • Hazard: a process, phenomenon or human activity that can cause damage.
  • Vulnerability: characteristics that increase susceptibility to the impact of hazards.
  • Exposure: people, infrastructure and assets located in hazard-prone areas.
Hazards + Vulnerability + Exposure = Disaster Risk

Disaster risk: the likelihood of loss of life, injury, or destruction and damage from a disaster in a given period.

Risk drivers

Climate change, environmental degradation, globalized economic development, poverty and inequality, poorly planned urban development, weak governance.

Cause and effect

Human actions (unsustainable consumption, negligence) worsen disaster risk. Disasters affect everyone but hit the most vulnerable hardest: food security, accessibility, shelter, further environmental damage.

Sendai Framework for DRR: 4 priorities

  • Understanding disaster risk
  • Strengthening disaster risk governance
  • Investing in DRR
  • Enhancing preparedness for effective response

The State has the primary role, but responsibility is shared with the private sector, communities and individuals.

SDG 2: Zero Hunger

End hunger, food security, nutrition, sustainable agriculture. 2.3: double the productivity and incomes of small-scale food producers. 2.4: resilient agriculture.

SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production

12.3: halve per capita food waste at retail and consumer levels. 12.5: reduce waste through prevention, recycling and reuse. 12.c: rationalize wasteful fossil-fuel subsidies.

SDG 13: Climate Action

13.1: resilience to climate hazards. 13.2: climate measures in national planning. 13.a: developed countries to mobilize $100 billion yearly by 2020; operationalize the Green Climate Fund.

Module 2.5 — SDGs 7 & 11

＋

SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy

Ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all.

  • 7.1: universal access to modern energy services.
  • 7.2: increase the share of renewable energy.
  • 7.3: double the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency.

SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities

Make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable.

  • 11.1: adequate, safe, affordable housing; upgrade slums.
  • 11.2: safe, accessible public transport.
  • 11.4: protect cultural and natural heritage.
  • 11.5: reduce deaths and losses from disasters.
  • 11.6: air quality and municipal waste management.
  • 11.7: safe, inclusive green and public spaces.
  • 11.b: holistic disaster risk management in line with the Sendai Framework 2015–2030.

Activity: design a sustainable city/community that would have reduced the effects of your group's assigned disaster.

Module 2.7 — Agents, SDGs 8, 9 & 17 · Security

＋

Agents for environmental protection

Government: LGUs, DENR, BFAR, Climate Change Commission. CSOs/NGOs: PMPI, Center for Environmental Concerns, Greenpeace, Haribon Foundation.

SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth

Sustained, inclusive growth and decent work. Highlights: growth through innovation and diversification, labor rights and ending forced and child labor, sustainable tourism, aid for trade.

SDG 9: Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure

Build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialization, foster innovation. Highlights: inclusive infrastructure, “clean” technologies, access to information.

SDG 17: Partnerships for the Goals

Strengthen means of implementation and revitalize the Global Partnership. Highlights: resource mobilization for developing countries, technology and trade cooperation, public-private partnerships.

Security

Two definitions: national/physical security and human security.

National security examples: Marawi before and after the siege; the Chinese water cannon attack on Filipino fishermen; escalating cyber threats linked to West Philippine Sea tensions (AFP Cyber Command).

Threats to human security come as shocks, trends and seasonalities, across six spheres: political, economic, social, environmental, technological, health.

Film: Poverty Inc.

Discussion question: can international aid also become a threat to national and human security?
